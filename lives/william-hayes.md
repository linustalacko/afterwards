In the first week of October 1842 a boy was going about London taking the boots off other boys. On Monday the 3rd it was Laurence Brennan’s son, also Laurence. On Thursday the 6th, at about eleven in the morning, it was William Hampshire. On Saturday the 8th it was John Tatnell, who told the court how it was done: “Hayes came and told me something—he took off my boots and ran away with them.”[^trial] What he told him is not recorded.

John Tatnell’s boots were worth three shillings. They were pawned the same day at a shop in Great Suffolk Street, Southwark, by a woman of twenty-one called Mary Ann Munday, who gave her name as Ann Thompson. When a constable asked her about the pawn ticket she said she had picked it up outside the street door. In court she said: “I pawned one pair of boots; Hayes said they were his own.”

The trial was on 24 October at the Central Criminal Court. William pleaded guilty to taking John Tatnell’s boots. The printed report gives his age as ten and puts a star beside his name, and the front of that month’s volume says what the star means: “prisoners have been previously in custody.”[^star] He had already served a month in gaol for stealing shoes.[^prior] The sentence was transportation for ten years. He was then tried for the other two pairs and found not guilty of both. Mary Ann Munday got six months.

![The page itself: the Old Bailey Proceedings for 24 October 1842, with the star beside his name. Image: Old Bailey Online, used non-commercially.](record-hayes-trial.jpg)

He sailed from Sheerness on 28 May 1843 in the convict ship *Asiatic* and landed at Hobart, in Van Diemen’s Land, 118 days later, on 23 September.[^ship] There a clerk wrote him down more thoroughly than anyone had before. Age, twelve. Height, four feet five and a quarter inches. Dark brown hair, dark hazel eyes, freckled, a scar on the right side of his forehead and another on his left cheek. He could read and write. He gave his native place as Oxford Street. Asked what he had been sent out for, he said eight pairs of shoes.[^indent] The report that came with him from England was one word: “Bad.”

And he was tattooed. On his right arm were a mermaid, a crucifixion, a fouled anchor and his initials; on his left, another anchor, the body of a man, and three dots.[^tattoo] I do not know where a boy of ten or twelve came by six tattoos: in London, in the seven months he waited in England after his trial, or at sea. They are the reason he can be followed at all.

For the next eight years I have nothing. Boys of his age who landed at Hobart in those years were usually sent to Point Puer, the boys’ prison beside Port Arthur. That is what generally happened, not something I have read about him: his conduct record in the Tasmanian archives will say where he went, and I could not open it.[^gap] He comes back into view in 1851, about nineteen, hired out in Hobart. In March he went to William Thompson of Macquarie Street at nine pounds a year; at the end of March to Martin Campbell of Davey Street, for a month, at the rate of twelve; in July to William Pettard of Elizabeth Street, for a year, at nine.[^labour] [George Baylis](../george-baylis/), who had landed five months before him, was hired to a mistress in Davey Street that May. Nothing says the two ever met. On 10 May 1852 he was given his ticket of leave, and on 1 November his certificate of freedom, with the remark “Sentence expired.”[^free] He was about twenty, and free, and on the far side of the world from Oxford Street.

![Knut Bull, View of Hobart Town, 1853. The town where William Hayes landed at about eleven and was given his freedom at about twenty, painted the year after.](hobart-1853.jpg)

Then come seventeen years with no record that I could find. A man who keeps out of court leaves less behind him, and the newspapers that might show him at work, or married, are closed to the way I read.

On 14 September 1869 the Supreme Court in Hobart sentenced William Hayes, of the *Asiatic*, to two years for indecent assault. The gazette that records it does not say who was assaulted, or how old they were. On 26 November 1870 he was given six months more for escaping. He was discharged on 24 April 1872, and the police gazette described him so that constables would know him again: thirty-eight, five feet four and a half, hair greyish, native of Essex, “W. H., anchor, crucifix, & mermaid, on right arm.”[^gazette72]

That description is how I know the man of 1869 is the boy of 1842. The ship is the same, and so are the initials, and the anchor, the crucifix and the mermaid are where the clerk at Hobart found them twenty-nine years earlier. He had grown eleven inches. Two things do not agree. In 1843 he said Oxford Street, and now the gazette says Essex: he may have been born in one and raised in the other, or a clerk may have been careless. And the three sets of records make him born in 1831, 1832 and 1834. I have used the Old Bailey’s age, which is the earliest.

After that the record is Launceston, and short sentences. April 1874: threatening, fourteen days. November 1874: idle and disorderly, a month. June 1875: threatening to stab, three months. February 1876, at Evandale: larceny, a month.[^gazette74] He was discharged from Launceston gaol on Wednesday 8 March 1876. The gazette put him at forty-two, five feet four, dark-haired, with the anchor and crucifix and a mermaid on each arm.

That is the last I can find of him. I do not know where he died, or whether anyone was with him.

Almost everything known about William Hayes was written down by people who had him in custody. They measured him six times. The only words of his that survive are a plea of guilty, the name of a street, and a number of shoes larger than the court ever charged him with. Three boys went home without their boots in October 1842, and in 1869 someone was assaulted whose name the record does not give. They belong in this library too, and are not in it yet.

## Records that are not him

The database that links these records attaches four more to his name. I have left them out of the life.

- **William Haynes**, who came to New South Wales in the *Asia* in 1822 and was recommended for a conditional pardon on 1 August 1840 (two index entries). Our William was about eight then, and in London.
- **A man discharged at Westbury** on 23 September 1865. The gazette gives his ship as the *Asia*, arrived 1836.
- **A man sentenced at Launceston** on 14 July 1874 to a month for resisting a constable. This one is doubtful, not disproved. The gazette gives his ship as the *Asiatic*, but makes him five feet seven and a native of Launceston, and lists no tattoos. William was measured at five feet three and three quarters in April and again in November. It may be a clerk’s slip or another William Hayes, and I have not counted it.

## Not yet known

- Where he was between 1843 and 1851, and whether he was at Point Puer. His conduct record in the Tasmanian archives will say.
- Who his parents were. A baptism between 1831 and 1834, near Oxford Street or in Essex, would be the place to start.
- What happened in Hobart in 1869. The newspapers of that September will have reported the Supreme Court.
- Anything at all between 1852 and 1869: work, a marriage, children.
- When and where he died. He was alive on 8 March 1876.
- Whether the man of July 1874 was him.
- What became of John Tatnell, William Hampshire and Laurence Brennan junior, and of Mary Ann Munday.
