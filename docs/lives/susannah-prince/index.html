First written down on 28 January 1730, at York Buildings, in the parish of St Martin in the Fields, London

Susannah Prince

about 1709 to 3 January 1741

At sixteen or seventeen she married James Prince, a man born in Jamaica who had been given away as a present when he was an infant. Twice she told the parish what she knew of his life and her own. Then the workhouse registers keep her, to the day she died.

On 28 January 1730 the parish of St Martin in the Fields, in London, examined a woman of about twenty who was lodging at Mrs Rich’s in York Buildings. A settlement examination was how a parish decided whether a poor person belonged to it and had to be kept by it; that is general, and her examination does not say why she was asked. Her name was Susannah Prince, and what she said is the first record of her I could find.1

She said she was the wife of James Prince, and that they had been married at the Fleet about four years before. He had no trade and had never been an apprentice. In the clerk’s words, “he is a Blackmoor and was born at Jamaica,” and he had been brought over to England by a Major Cosley, with whom, as the transcription reads, he had lived thirty-six years. The Major lodged at a Mrs Dean’s at Charing Cross, and James had lived with him there about four years before the marriage. Then the Major went to Scotland, and James with him, and when he came back to England he lodged at Mrs Dean’s again. He had six pounds a year, with his diet and lodging, and stayed in that service until six months before. She had no child, “but is now great wth Child.”1

Nearly eight years later, on 15 December 1737, she was examined again. She was now twenty-eight, in the workhouse, and the widow of James Prince, “who dyed nine weeks ago.” Nine weeks before the examination was 13 October 1737.2

This time the account is longer, and it differs from the first. They had been married “at the hand and Pen at the ffleet eleven years ago.” When she married him he was a servant to a Captain Cossly, who lodged at Mrs Deane’s, a pewterer’s, at Charing Cross. And the clerk wrote this:

“being a Negroe he was given as a Present to Major Cossly of Vitners in Kent when an Infant whom he served to his Death and then he served the said Captn Cossley Son of the said Major till she this Examinant marryed him.”2

2 St Martin in the Fields, settlement examination of Susan Prince, 15 December 1737. London Lives, dataset record smdsset_25_2583; the volume is given as F5029, item 273. A transcription; I saw no image of the page. The interval of nine weeks was computed by program.

She did not know what wages he had before the marriage. Afterwards, she said, he had seven pounds a year. She herself had been a yearly hired servant to Dr Misaubin in St Martin’s Lane, for upwards of a year, at seven pounds a year with her diet and lodging, and she had married from his house. Neither of them had ever kept house at a rent of ten pounds a year or paid parish taxes. Below the examination, in the transcription, is one more line: “James Prince had no Wages at all of Capt Cossley certain John Godley.” I think it is someone, perhaps a John Godley, telling the parish that James had been paid nothing by the Captain, but that is a guess.2

That is nearly everything I could read of James Prince: what his wife said of him, twice, to a parish clerk. He was born in Jamaica, given away when he was an infant, served a father and then his son, married at the Fleet, and died in October 1737. I have not found his burial.

The Cossleys can be glimpsed in the National Archives’ catalogue, and only in its descriptions; I have not seen the letters. On 11 April 1718 a “Major Nat [Nathaniel] Cossley” wrote to Charles Delafaye at Whitehall to thank him; the letter, the catalogue says, mentions the cold weather and the failure of the fruit crop, and is endorsed “vintners.”3 I think he is the Major of “Vitners in Kent” to whom James was given, but nothing in the letter, as described, mentions James. On 8 May 1725 a “Capt Cossley” wrote to the same man about the march to Elgin to try to disarm the Highlanders.4 Susannah’s first examination says the Major went to Scotland and took James with him, and her second makes the Captain the master James served before the marriage. If the Captain of 1725 is that son, James may have been with him in Scotland that spring, a year or so before he married. The records I read do not join them.

Her own master, Dr Misaubin, was dead by October 1734: the catalogue lists the will of John Misaubin, “Doctor and Practitioner in Physic,” of St Martin in the Fields, proved on the 22nd of that month.5

5 The National Archives, PROB 11/667/317, will of John Misaubin, 22 October 1734. The catalogue’s description only.

From December 1737 the workhouse registers of St Martin’s take her up.6 She had been admitted on 8 December, a week before her second examination. The entry reads “Itch Rt” and then the overseer, Mr Hume; I take “Rt” to mean recommended by. She was discharged on 14 February 1738. On 16 April 1739 she was admitted again; where the first entry has “Itch”, this one reads “ye Gentlemen being at ye. House.” She left in May, on the 7th by one register and the 17th by the other. On 4 October 1739 she came in again, with the overseer Mr Hubert’s name beside the entry, and left on 9 November. On 7 December 1739 she came in once more, and was discharged on 26 March 1740.

On 2 January 1741, with the overseer Mr Churchman’s name beside the entry, she was admitted for the fifth time; the second register numbers her admissions, and this is number five. Against the next day, 3 January 1741, both registers have one word: “Dyed.” The registers give her age as thirty-one.7

Before 1752 the English year began on 25 March, so a register written at the time might have called this January 1740; that is general. Counting from the registers’ dates, she spent 246 days in the workhouse in those three years, or 236 if she left in May 1739 on the 7th. Neither the second examination nor any register names a child of hers. Whether the child she was carrying in January 1730 was born, and lived, I have not found.

Records that are not her

  • Anne Prince, baptised at St Paul, Covent Garden, on 26 April 1730, daughter of George and Susanna Prince (FreeREG, a transcript). The date and the mother’s name fit the child Susannah was carrying three months earlier, and Covent Garden is next to St Martin’s. But the father is George, and her husband was James. Not hers, unless the register or the transcript is wrong.
  • Sarah Prince, in the same workhouse from September 1739 to 1748, aged twenty-nine in 1739, who died there on 10 March 1748 aged thirty-seven (smdswhr_127_12743, smdswhr_254_25408). She was in the house at the same time as Susannah in October 1739, her admissions are counted separately, and she lived on after Susannah’s death. Another woman. Whether they were kin, nothing says.
  • Susannah Prince, wife of John Prince, then in the King’s sea service, removed from St Martin’s with two children on 8 September 1779 (LMSMPS50716PS507160070). Thirty-eight years after Susannah’s death.
  • Amey, John and Elizabeth Prince, who died in the same workhouse in 1741, 1745 and 1749. Nothing ties them to her.

Where the records disagree

  • How James came to the Cossleys. In 1730 he was “brot over to England” by the Major. In 1737 he was given to the Major as a present when he was an infant.
  • The thirty-six years. The 1730 transcription says he lived with the Major thirty-six years. That would make him at least thirty-six in 1730, and more than twice her age. I cannot check the figure against the page.
  • His wages. Six pounds a year, with diet and lodging, in 1730. Seven pounds a year after the marriage, in 1737, and a line beneath: “no Wages at all of Capt Cossley.”
  • Her age in 1739. Thirty-one in the running register in April 1739, twenty-nine and a half in the other; twenty-nine and three-quarters in October. She was twenty-eight in December 1737.
  • The day she left in May 1739. The 7th in one register, the 17th in the other.

Not yet known

  • The Fleet register entry for the marriage, about 1726, at the Hand and Pen. The Fleet registers are at the National Archives (RG 7); their indexes are on subscription sites.
  • James Prince’s burial, about October 1737, and hers, in January 1741. The burial registers of St Martin in the Fields were not read.
  • The child she was expecting in January 1730: whether it was born, baptised, and lived.
  • Where she was born, and who her parents were. Neither examination says.
  • The original examination books and workhouse registers, of which I read transcriptions only: the “thirty-six years”, the “3d Major”, and the line about John Godley should be read on the page.
  • Who Major Nathaniel Cossley was, where “Vitners” is, and when he died. The letters SP 35/74/47 and SP 54/15/17 themselves.
  • Which son was the Captain. The catalogue has a Captain John Cossley made a major in Colonel Duroure’s regiment in 1742 (SP 41/13/116), and a Terence Cossley in 1745 (C 11/2106/14). Nothing I read joins either to James.
  • How James came from Jamaica. A John Cossley “of Hinston in the Island of Jamaica” left a will in 1703 (PROB 11/472/432). Whether he was of the same family, and whether he had anything to do with an infant born there, is not known.
  • Who John Godley was.
  • What she died of.
